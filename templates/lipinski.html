Lipinski Rule of Five Calculator

Check whether a molecule is likely to be orally active. Enter a SMILES and the calculator evaluates Lipinski's rule of five plus the Veber extension for polar surface area and flexibility.

Multiple molecules are allowed — one per line.

What is Lipinski's rule of five?

Christopher Lipinski observed in 1997 that most orally administered drugs satisfy four simple physicochemical limits. A compound that violates more than one of them is statistically less likely to reach the market. The “five” comes from the fact that every threshold is a multiple of five.

RuleLimitWhy it matters
Molecular weight≤ 500 DaLarge molecules permeate membranes poorly
logP (lipophilicity)≤ 5Too lipophilic means poor aqueous solubility
Hydrogen bond donors≤ 5Each donor costs energy to desolvate
Hydrogen bond acceptors≤ 10Same, for acceptors

The usual interpretation is “no more than one violation”. Two or more violations is where the probability of poor oral absorption rises sharply.

How this calculator works

Paste a SMILES, MOL, SDF, PDB or InChI structure. OpenBabel computes molecular weight, logP, hydrogen-bond donors, hydrogen-bond acceptors, TPSA and rotatable bonds directly from the structure. The page then compares each value with the Lipinski and Veber thresholds and shows which rules pass and which fail. No external database or lookup table is involved, so the result is reproducible from the structure alone.

Multiple molecules are accepted — one per line. For a full descriptor table rather than a pass/fail summary, use the molecular property calculator. For a library, use the batch converter or the properties API.

Veber rules

Veber et al. (2002) added two more predictors of oral bioavailability, which this calculator also reports:

  • TPSA ≤ 140 Å² — polar surface area above this usually means poor absorption.
  • Rotatable bonds ≤ 10 — more flexibility means a higher conformational entropy cost on binding.

A molecule can pass Lipinski and fail Veber — a long flexible chain with one polar group, for example. Reporting both gives a more honest picture.

Common mistakes and limitations

  • The rule is a filter, not a verdict. Passing it does not make a compound a drug; failing it does not make a compound useless.
  • logP is not logD. The rule uses the neutral-form partition coefficient. At physiological pH, an ionisable acid or base has a different effective lipophilicity. If you are reasoning about absorption, logD is often the more relevant number.
  • HBA counting is easy to get wrong. Lipinski's acceptor count is the sum of nitrogen and oxygen atoms, not the number of lone pairs or acceptor groups. A nitro group counts as three.
  • Natural products and PROTACs routinely violate the rule and are still biologically active. The thresholds were derived from synthetic oral drug libraries.
  • Salts and counter-ions change the molecular weight. Strip them if you want to evaluate the parent compound.

Frequently asked questions

Does passing the rule of five guarantee an oral drug?

No. The rule is a cheap triage filter based on historical distributions. Many approved drugs violate one rule, and many rule-compliant compounds fail in clinical trials.

What counts as a violation?

Each property is compared independently. A value beyond the threshold is one violation. The usual guidance is that two or more violations predict poor oral absorption; one violation is common and not automatically disqualifying.

How are hydrogen bond donors and acceptors counted?

Donors are N–H and O–H groups. Acceptors are nitrogen and oxygen atoms. The calculator uses OpenBabel's definitions, which follow the standard Lipinski interpretation.

Can I check a whole library?

Yes. Paste multiple SMILES, one per line, or use the batch converter. For large libraries, call the API and apply the thresholds in your own spreadsheet.

Why does my logP differ from another calculator?

logP is predicted, not measured. Different implementations (OpenBabel, RDKit, Crippen, XLogP) use different atom contributions and can differ by a few tenths. The pass/fail result is usually stable near the threshold, but borderline compounds should be checked with an experimental value.

Are the rules applied to natural products?

Not reliably. Natural products, peptides and PROTACs often fall outside the rule-of-five space yet are biologically active. Use the rule as one signal among several, not as a universal gate.
