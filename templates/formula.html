Molecular Weight Calculator

Calculate average molecular weight and elemental composition from a chemical formula or a SMILES string. Includes worked examples, common molecular weights and the difference between average and monoisotopic mass.

Supports brackets and multipliers: (NH4)2SO4, Ca(OH)2

What this molecular weight calculator does

Enter a formula such as C6H12O6 or a structure in SMILES, MOL, SDF, PDB or InChI format. The calculator returns the average molecular weight in g/mol, the Hill-notation formula, the total atom count and the elemental composition as a mass percentage for each element.

For logP, TPSA, hydrogen-bond donors and acceptors, and rotatable bonds, use the molecular property calculator. For 3D coordinates, use SMILES to 3D. To convert between chemical file formats, use the chemical format converter.

How molecular weight is calculated

The calculator sums the IUPAC standard atomic weights of every atom in the formula. This is the average molecular weight — the value used for concentration calculations (for example, "dissolve 1 mmol in 1 mL"). For glucose:

C6H12O6 = 6 × 12.011 + 12 × 1.008 + 6 × 15.999
        = 72.066 + 12.096 + 95.994
        = 180.156 g/mol

When you enter a structure, the formula is first perceived by OpenBabel and then summed. Explicit hydrogens, salts and disconnected fragments are included in the total.

Average molecular weight vs monoisotopic mass

Average molecular weight uses the natural abundance of each isotope. It is the number you want when weighing a sample, preparing a solution or reporting a yield. Monoisotopic mass uses the most abundant isotope of each element — for example, 12C is taken as 12.000000 rather than 12.011. It is the value used for high-resolution mass spectrometry and for matching a measured m/z to a formula.

For small molecules the two values are close: glucose is 180.156 g/mol on average and 180.0634 Da monoisotopic. The gap grows with molecule size and with the number of heavy atoms. If you are checking an MS result, do not compare it with the average molecular weight. For a longer explanation and worked examples, see the average vs monoisotopic mass guide.

Formula syntax

  • C6H12O6 — glucose
  • (NH4)2SO4 — ammonium sulfate; brackets are multiplied out
  • Ca(OH)2 — calcium hydroxide
  • CuSO4(H2O)5 — copper(II) sulfate pentahydrate; write hydrates with brackets
  • Element symbols are case sensitive: Co is cobalt, CO is carbon monoxide

Charges and radicals are not part of the formula syntax. A charged species has the same molecular weight as its neutral form unless the number of atoms changes.

Frequently asked questions

How do you calculate molecular weight from a formula?

Multiply the atomic weight of each element by the number of atoms of that element in the formula, then add the results. For glucose, six carbon atoms contribute 72.066, twelve hydrogens contribute 12.096 and six oxygens contribute 95.994, for a total of 180.156 g/mol.

Can I calculate molecular weight from a SMILES string?

Yes. Switch to “From structure”, paste the SMILES and run the calculation. The structure is converted to a formula first, so salts, explicit hydrogens and disconnected fragments are included in the total.

Why does the formula change after conversion?

OpenBabel outputs the formula in Hill notation: carbon first, hydrogen second, then all other elements alphabetically. H8N2O4S and (NH4)2SO4 describe the same compound.

Does it account for charge or isotopes?

No — the calculation treats every atom as its neutral, naturally abundant isotope. Use a dedicated monoisotopic mass calculator for isotope-labelled compounds or high-resolution mass spectrometry.

What is the difference between molecular weight and molar mass?

For practical purposes they are the same number. Molecular weight is dimensionless and is often reported in daltons (Da); molar mass is the mass of one mole and is reported in g/mol. This calculator reports g/mol.

Why is the molecular weight of a protein different?

Proteins are polymers of amino acids, so their mass is the sum of residue masses plus water. A formula string is not a practical way to enter a protein; use the protein molecular weight calculator with the amino-acid sequence instead.
